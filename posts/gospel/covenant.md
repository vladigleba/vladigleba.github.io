In the previous article, we learned about four different expulsions: the first of Lucifer and his followers from the heavenly Eden, the second of Adam and Eve from the earthly one, and the third of the Canaanites, and later, the Israelites, from the land of Canaan. A fourth one is yet future, when the brightness of Christ’s second coming destroys the lost across the whole earth {% rf '2 Thessalonians 1:8–9; 2:8' %}, leaving it barren and desolate for the first time since creation week. These expulsions may seem harsh or arbitrary on their own, but when examined within a larger context, they provide greater insight into God’s character and His designs for His creation.

When Lucifer’s rebellion took a third of the angels with it, God’s heavenly kingdom was left with a void. Pharaoh had a similar experience, though on a much smaller scale, when the Israelites left his country the night of the exodus. Overnight, a vast number of positions and responsibilities essential to the Egyptian kingdom but left vacant by the Israelites’ departure, needed to be filled by someone else. Did the Egyptians ever recover? Would God ever recover?

> What is man, that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him? For *thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour. Thou madest him to have dominion over the works of thy hands; thou hast put all things under his feet*.
{% ct 'Psalm 8:4-6' %}

When God created Adam, He began a new order of beings, created in His own image {% rf 'Genesis 1:26' %}, “a little lower than the angels.” Adam was a king, for he was crowned with glory and honor, and because he was a king, the earth was the dominion over which he ruled. After God created Eve, He blessed the pair and commanded them to populate and subdue the earth, to transform it into a garden of Eden. 

After losing a third of His angels, it was natural for God to desire to fill that void. But it is noteworthy that He not create additional angels to replace the fallen ones: He created a new order of beings in His own image, different from the angels, beings that would one day take the place of the fallen ones.[^1] Unlike humans, angels do not have the ability to procreate {% rf 'Matthew 22:30' %} or to rule over their own dominion; instead of being rulers, they are God’s ministers {% rf 'Psalm 103:21; Hebrews 1:14' %}. Humanity, on the other hand, had the creative capacity to bear children and to rule. Because of this, we would understand, more than any other created order, the God who was misunderstood by a third of His angels. Humanity, and the universe through their experience, would eventually recognize that God’s government is just, and that laws are indispensable to secure order and happiness for all.

> But God hath chosen the foolish things of the world to confound the wise; and God hath chosen the *weak things of the world to confound the things which are mighty*.
{% ct '1 Corinthians 1:27' %}

> O LORD our Lord, how excellent is thy name in all the earth! who hast set thy glory above the heavens. *Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength because of thine enemies, that thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger*.
{% ct 'Psalm 8:1-2' %}

Because of their rebellion, humanity would give God an opportunity to silence Satan, the enemy and the avenger, through the power of love. As God spent time with Adam and Eve on the seventh day of creation week, walking them through a tour of His wondrous Eden garden, He knew that they would turn their back on Him. He knew that He would die because of them. But He also knew that it would be worth it. Because after He had redeemed them, He would finally have rest from Lucifer’s [war against Him](/posts/gospel/unrest.md). When their descendants would crucify Christ on the cross, His death would be the means by which He would reconcile all to Himself and, thus, put an end to sin—forever {% rf 'John 12:32; Ephesians 1:10' %}.

Because redeemed humanity would understand Him better than any angel, He would even let them judge the fallen angels, including Lucifer himself, [during the heavenly millennium](/posts/prophecy/millennium.md#what-do-the-saved-do-in-heaven) {% rf '1 Corinthians 6:3' %}. This was, in fact, His original plan for Adam and Eve. 

> If a false witness rise up against any man to testify against him that which is wrong; then both the men, between whom the controversy is, shall stand before the LORD, before the priests and the judges, which shall be in those days; and *the judges shall make diligent inquisition*.
{% ct 'Deuteronomy 19:16-18' %}.

In heaven, Satan [became a false witness](/posts/gospel/evil.md) against God {% rf 'John 8:44' %} after he started believing he could do a better job in running the universe {% rf 'Isaiah 14:12–14' %}. Faced with an accuser who was fast gaining a following, how would God respond? He could have immediately destroy Satan and his followers for their audacity to rebel. But that is not His character. He seeks to save, not to destroy {% rf 'Luke 9:56' %}. No doubt, God would have appealed to Lucifer again and again to turn around, for He is long-suffering, “not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance.” But Lucifer eventually crossed the line of no return. He refused to reconcile.

Instead of demonstrating His power over Lucifer, God respected his choice. But He went even further than that: He opened Himself up to judgment. Lucifer had raised serious accusations against His character and government, and a neutral third party, a jury, was needed to settle the case.

In order to be part of the jury, three requirements must be met:
1. be a law-abiding citizen,
1. have no firsthand knowledge of the conflict,
1. be able to differentiate between good and evil.
Adam and Eve met all three: God created them in His own image as law-abiding citizens, they did not witness Lucifer’s rebellion, for they were created after it occurred, and God was clear on what they could and could not do.[^2]

To be fair to Satan, God placed two trees in the midst of the garden: the tree of life and tree of knowledge of good and evil {% rf 'Genesis 2:9' %}. They represented two choices: Him or Satan. Though Adam and Eve had no reason to turn on their Creator, for their life was perfect, the freedom to do so was nonetheless there.

The day came when Eve wandered near the forbidden tree, and Satan knew his opportunity had come. He disguised himself as a serpent and presented his side of the story, accusing God of being manipulative and untrustworthy. Eve considered his arguments and decided that he was more trustworthy than God. Adam soon followed her lead. After securing a third of God’s angels, Satan had now deceived humanity, the very ones who were to judge him.

[^1]: How few realize our high and noble calling and accept a dehumanizing worldview in its place that suggests humanity accidentally evolved from primordial goo, devoid of meaning or purpose. What would the biblical worldview do for the hopeless and despondent among us? What would the realization that our earthly life is training for heavenly responsibilities do for all of us? How much meaning would that bring to the most mundane or the most challenging occupations? Adam certainly did not have it easy after the fall. But God in His wisdom saw that Him fallen nature needed to wrestle with work that included sweat, thorns, and thistles {% rf 'Genesis 3:18–19' %}. If we are open, even unpleasant work can teach us lessons for eternity.

## How did God respond?

Because Satan used deception to disqualify the jury, God gave them a second chance—at infinite cost to Himself. An animal (likely a lamb) died that day, because God later clothed them in animal skins, after their fig leaves proved inadequate, a fitting symbol of our failed attempts to cover our spiritual nakedness through our own works {% rf 'Genesis 3:21; Revelation 16:15' %}. The animal skins were a tangible confirmation of His earlier promise that He would wage war against Satan and overcome him {% rf 'Genesis 3:15' %}.

Thus, our Creator proclaimed the gospel to our first parents the same day they betrayed Him. Instead of abandoning them in their despair, or starting over with a new creation, He yoked Himself to them by a covenant promise: a coming Deliverer would die the death they should have died. And He would cover their spiritual nakedness with His own perfect righteousness. If God treated them with such mercy, would He treat us, their descendants, any differently? He is the same yesterday, and today, and forever {% rf 'Hebrews 13:8' %}.

## To whom was the Edenic promise later confirmed?

After Abraham’s father, Terah, died in Haran, God appeared to Abraham when he was seventy-five years old and commanded him to leave Haran for Canaan {% rf 'Genesis 12:1–4' %}. As part of the command, God included a promise that He would bless Abraham and that in him would “all families of the earth be blessed.” What was this blessing?

> For what saith the scripture? Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness. Now to him that worketh is the reward not reckoned of grace, but of debt. But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness. Even as David also describeth the blessedness of the man, unto whom God imputeth righteousness without works, saying, *Blessed are they whose iniquities are forgiven, and whose sins are covered. Blessed is the man to whom the Lord will not impute sin.*
{% ct 'Romans 4:3-8' %}

Paul identifies God’s blessing as the blessing of sins forgiven, and Peter confirms it:

> Ye are the children of the prophets, and of the covenant which God made with our fathers, saying unto Abraham, And in thy seed shall all the kindreds of the earth be blessed. Unto you first God, *having raised up his Son Jesus, sent him to bless you, in turning away every one of you from his iniquities*.
{% ct 'Acts 3:25-26' %}

Peter declares that the blessing goes beyond the mere forgiveness of sin to include a turning away from it and that Jesus is at the center of it. Christ is the One who fulfills the {% rf 'Genesis 3:15' false %} promise by reconciling humanity back to Himself, thus healing the great rift created by Adam and Eve’s fall. The entrance of sin meant that the first pair could no longer commune with God face to face without being consumed by His holiness {% rf 'Hebrews 12:29' %}; instead, they hid themselves when He came calling for them {% rf 'Genesis 3:8' %}. Why did they feel the need to hide? God explained the reason to Moses when he asked to see His glory, “Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live” {% rf 'Exodus 33:20' %}. Centuries later, the prophet Isaiah clarified it further: “But your iniquities have separated between you and your God, and your sins have hid his face from you” {% rf 'Isaiah 59:2' %}. Thus, resolving the sin problem lies at the core of God’s blessing to Abraham, and Christ is the only means to its fulfillment. Abraham needed Christ then as much as we do now.

> Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree: that *the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ*; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.
{% ct 'Galatians 3:13-14' %}

The blessing of God’s covenant with Abraham is the blessing of the gospel, the good news of Christ’s victory over sin on our behalf. Abraham received that gospel blessing by faith alone—as we do today.

> For the promise, that he should be the heir of the world, was not to Abraham, or to his seed, through the law, but *through the righteousness of faith*.
{% ct 'Romans 4:13' %}

Another synonym for the turning away from sin is righteousness. Abraham believed that despite his sinfulness, God would make Him righteous in Christ. Even though he did not know how it would be done, he did not doubt. Had he doubted, he would have forfeited the blessing of righteousness. Zacharias, the father of John the Baptist, recognized that God’s covenant with Abraham included righteousness when he prophesied of the coming Messiah.

> …to remember his holy covenant; the oath which he swore *to our father Abraham*, that he would grant unto us, that we being delivered out of the hand of our enemies might serve him without fear, *in holiness and righteousness before him*, all the days of our life.
{% ct 'Luke 1:72-75' %}

God’s promise to Abraham was repeated by Zacharias thousands of years later as the Hope of that covenant was about to be born. John the Baptist recognized Him too when he declared, “Behold the Lamb of God, which taketh away the sin of the world” {% rf 'John 1:29' %}. He was the Lamb in whom Abraham placed his hope when he prophesied to his son Isaac, “My son, God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering” {% rf 'Genesis 22:8' %}. God did indeed provide Himself on a cruel Roman cross.

That covenant with Abraham thousands of years ago has not lost any of its relevancy today. His promise of righteousness by faith is still available to us, because it is an everlasting covenant {% rf 'Hebrews 13:20' %}. We can receive the same blessings that Abraham received in Christ by faith.

## What else did God’s promise include?

Twenty-four years after speaking to Abraham in Haran, God spoke again. Abraham was ninety-nine years old and Sarah could no longer bear children, yet God gave him an astounding promise:

> Neither shall thy name any more be called Abram, but thy name shall be Abraham; for *a father of many nations have I made thee*. And I will make thee exceeding fruitful, and I will make nations of thee, and kings shall come out of thee. And *I will establish my covenant between me and thee and thy seed after thee in their generations for an everlasting covenant, to be a God unto thee, and to thy seed after thee*. And I will give unto thee, and to thy seed after thee, the land wherein thou art a stranger, *all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession*; and I will be their God.
{% ct 'Genesis 17:5-8' %}

God’s everlasting covenant with Abraham included an everlasting possession of land. But two chapters earlier, when Abraham had asked God, “How shall I know that I will inherit it?” God told him the following: 

> Know of a surety that thy seed shall be a stranger in a land that is not theirs, and shall serve them; and they shall afflict them four hundred years… And *thou shalt go to thy fathers in peace; thou shalt be buried in a good old age*. But in the fourth generation they shall come hither again: for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full.
{% ct 'Genesis 15:13, 15-16' %}

In other words, Abraham would die before he would set foot in the land of promise. Yet two chapters later, God told Him, “And I will give unto thee, and to thy seed after thee, the land wherein thou art a stranger, all the land of Canaan, for an everlasting possession” {% rf 'Genesis 17:8' %}. Was God contradicting Himself? Stephen recognized this too:

> And he gave him none inheritance in it, no, *not so much as to set his foot on: yet he promised that he would give it to him for a possession*, and to his seed after him, when as yet he had no child.
{% ct 'Acts 7:5' %}

Since Abraham received the promise of righteousness by faith, the same is true of the land. It could only be possessed by faith, and it was: “By faith the walls of Jericho fell down” {% rf 'Hebrews 11:30' %}. However, the promised land was not limited to Canaan.

> For the promise, that he should be the *heir of the world*, was not to Abraham, or to his seed, through the law, but through the righteousness of faith.
{% ct 'Romans 4:13' %}

God’s promise regarding the land of Canaan included the possession of the entire earth. Christ reconfirmed this in His Sermon on the Mount: “Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth” {% rf 'Matthew 5:5' %}. Abraham’s descendants did eventually inherit the promised land of Canaan under the leadership of Joshua, but they never inherited the world. Even their Canaan inheritance, however, fell short of God’s ideal, for at the height of David’s rule, when God gave them a nation of their own and rest from all their enemies {% rf '2 Samuel 7:1' %}, He gave David a peculiar promise.

> I will appoint a place for My people Israel, and will plant them, *that they may dwell in a place of their own*, and move no more; *neither shall the children of wickedness afflict them* anymore as beforetime.
{% ct '2 Samuel 7:10' %}

At a time when David’s kingdom was most prosperous, God, nonetheless, expounded on His original promise to Abraham. David himself acknowledged this. As he publicly transferred the kingdom to Solomon, his son, David prayed, “For we are strangers [foreigners] before Thee, and sojourners [pilgrims], as were all our fathers; our days on the earth are as a shadow, and there is none abiding” {% rf '1 Chronicles 29:15' %}. David compared Israel's condition to that of their fathers: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Gideon, Samson, Jephthah, and Samuel, who "received not the promise" {% rf 'Hebrews 11:32-39' %}. He knew that the earthly Canaan was only a type that pointed to a greater reality--the heavenly Canaan.

Nor was Israel's Canaan inheritance an everlasting one. After Solomon’s kingdom split, the Assyrians conquered the Kingdom of Israel after besieging its capital, Samaria, while the Babylonians conquered the Kingdom of Judah after besieging Jerusalem. In both cases, they drove the inhabitants off their land. The descendants from Judah did eventually return from exile under the leadership of Zerubbabel, Ezra, and Nehemiah, only to be dispossessed again under the Roman Empire.

But there is another part to the possession of the promised land. An everlasting inheritance can only be enjoyed by people who have everlasting life. Abraham died before he inherited the promised land. Thus, he can possess it only through the resurrection, when death will be vanquished forever and eternal life will become a reality {% rf '1 Corinthians 15:51–55' %}. In order to have the gift of everlasting life, however, one must be righteous. For if God expelled Adam and Eve from the garden for the sin of eating the forbidden fruit, it is obvious that He will admit only the righteous into heaven. But we have no righteousness of our own, for all of us have sinned {% rf 'Isaiah 64:6; Romans 3:23' %}. If any of us enter heaven, therefore, it will be only because of Someone else's righteousness.

> For all the promises of God *in him are yea, and in him Amen*, unto the glory of God by us.
{% ct '2 Corinthians 1:20' %}

Christ is the cornerstone and the surety to all of God's promises.

> Wherefore remember, that ye being in time past Gentiles in the flesh, who are called Uncircumcision by that which is called the Circumcision in the flesh made by hands; that at that time ye were without Christ, being aliens from the commonwealth of Israel, and strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope, and without God in the world. But now in Christ Jesus ye who sometimes were far off are made nigh by the blood of Christ.
{% ct 'Ephesians 2:11-13' %}
